## But first, what is Rhythm Heaven, exactly?

Rhythm Heaven is probably one of the most unique franchises Nintendo has in their portfolio of IP. As the title implies, it's a rhythm game, but it's a very different kind of rhythm game.

When you think of rhythm games, what comes to mind? You probably think about the likes of Dance Dance Revolution or Guitar Hero, games where you have to press a corresponding button in time to an on-screen display. Most rhythm games, to my knowledge, are some variation of this format, and they're a ton of fun. The genre as a whole typically has an incredibly high skill ceiling, so watching a highly skilled rhythm game player mop the floor with a difficult chart can be incredibly mesmerizing.

Rhythm Heaven, however, doesn't abide by this formula, as there's no on-screen display telling you when to press a button. Instead, you're thrown into a collection of wacky minigames where anything can happen, but everything that does happen is done to the beat of a song.

For example, in one of Groove's early minigames, Disc Dog, you have to count 7 beats between the disc being thrown and pressing the A button to catch it. Without music, this would be incredibly tricky to time, but by counting along to the beat of the song, it becomes much easier! That's one of the simpler examples, but it demonstrates the essence of what Rhythm Heaven is all about, and what makes it unique.

So, put simply, Rhythm Heaven is a series of wildly different minigames that all share a common throughline: that the actions of the character you control are done in time to a rhythm, aided by various visual and audio cues. That's how I would describe it, at least. I find that it can be difficult to fully grasp what Rhythm Heaven is until you've tried it for yourself, so I'd recommened trying out the free demo of Groove on the eShop if you're on the fence... after you finish reading this review, of course >:D.

## The Solo Games: The Meat of the Package

Now that we've established the basics of what Rhythm Heaven is, let's dive into the package's main attraction: the Rhythm Games. Every Rhythm Heaven game has its own selection, and Groove features a total of 80 in its 'Play Solo' mode. Though... that number is a *bit* misleading, as it includes Remixs and sequel games. 

Still, it's a ton of content, probably the most in the entire series. The only game I would imagine comes close is Megamix, which is primarily made up of old content, whereas most everything in Groove is brand new. Even Groove's take on series staple minigame Karate Man, called Quick Hands, is different and new!

The structure of this mode sees the rhythm games divided into a bunch of groupings of five games, each with four individual games and a remix that mashes those four games together. You progress through the games one at a time, unlocking the next one upon earning a Good/OK rank or higher.

The game is also split into two halves: the Frontside, which houses the first 40 rhythm games, and the Flipside, housing the last 40. The Flipside is primarily home to sequels to the games on the Frontside, but also contains the last few new rhythm games, some of which are among the game's most memorable in my opinion.

As you progress, you'll gradually unlock a wide array of side modes, with the selection differing between the Frontside and Flipside meus, by reaching certain points in the game or collecting enough medals. And that is where the Rhythm Heaven series' massive replay value starts to show itself.

While you can have plenty of fun just playing through all the rhythm games once, I did find it to be fairly easy. I don't think there was a single game in Groove I had to retry. Thankfully, the game offers a number of ways to put your skills to the test. The first of these is the rating system. Depending on how you perform in a rhythm game, you'll recieve a rating of either "Try Again", "Good/OK", or "Amazing/Superb". If you perform particularly well and earn an "Amazing/Superb" on a game, you'll receive a medal. As mentioned before, new pieces of side content are unlocked as you collect more medals, offering incentive to go for them all. Plus, it's just really satisfying to see the menu fill up with medal icons.

Want something even more challenging? Well, every now and then, a randomly selected game you've previously cleared will offer you the opportunity to "Go for a Perfect". As you might expect, this requires you to hit every input in a given game without missing or being offbeat a single time. And the kicker? You can only try three times on a given game before the Perfect opportunity disappears or moves to another game. Plus, performing perfectly in a game while it's not highlighted won't earn you anything. You *have* to Perfect the game while it is specifically highlighted. I wasn't sure how to feel about this at first, but to my knowledge, this is how Perfects have always worked in Rhythm Heaven. Plus, I must say that I do like the added tension introduced by the limited opportunity, and how much more satisfying it makes earning a Perfect versus if you could keep trying as much as you wanted.

While playing through and clearing all the rhythm games isn't too hard, these extra challenges make Rhythm Heaven Groove, and, really, the series as a whole, the epitomy of 'easy to pickup, hard to master'.

One more note I want to make on difficulty is that, after going back and playing Rhythm Heaven Fever, Groove seems noticeably easier by comparison. I'm not sure if this is down to the game selection or perhaps the ranking system being more lenient, but, as I mentioned earlier, I didn't have to retry a single rhythm game in Groove. On the contrary, I had to retry multiple games multiple times in Fever. I could totally understand if a veteran of the series was disappointed by that lack of difficulty, but on the contrary, it does make Groove an excellent place to start if you're new to Rhythm Heaven. It was my first Rhythm Heaven game, and I felt it was great for getting my feet under me before jumping into something more difficult like Fever.
